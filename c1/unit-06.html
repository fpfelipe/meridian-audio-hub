AUDIO_C1_U06_T01 **Unit:** C1-U06 (Aesthetics of Cinema, Architecture & Sound: The Acoustic Space) ```text HOST: Welcome to Sound & Space. With us are Dr. Florian Vogel, principal acoustic engineer for the new Alpine Philharmonic Hall, and Sofia Rossi, whose minimalist feature Sotto Voce won international acclaim at Venice. Dr. Vogel, let us talk about reverberation time.

DR. FLORIAN VOGEL: In symphonic architecture, reverberation is not mere echo; it is spatial breath. For our alpine hall, what we targeted was an RT of precisely 2.0 seconds at mid-frequencies. If the decay drops below 1.8, the strings lose their luscious bloom; if it extends beyond 2.2, complex contrapuntal woodwind passages degenerate into acoustic soup. It was the introduction of micro-perforated oak diffusers that allowed us to sculpt that precise decay curve.

SOFIA ROSSI: That concept of "spatial breath" resonates profoundly with cinematography, Florian. In Sotto Voce, we set the entire second act in an abandoned subterranean limestone cistern in Palermo. Instead of relying on conventional visual framing, it was through the sound design that we sculpted the claustrophobia. We recorded the subsonic drone of municipal water mains three kilometers away and hyper-amplified the dry scrape of wool against damp stone. The reverberance of that damp cistern tells the audience that the protagonist is trapped long before she speaks.

DR. FLORIAN VOGEL: Precisely. The space itself becomes the primary actor. And what makes that acoustic presence so devastating is the punctuation of silence. When the drone cuts out, the listener’s eardrum actively reaches into the void. Silence in architecture, as in cinema, is never empty; it is pregnant with the ghost of previous resonance. ```
