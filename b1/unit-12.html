Unit 12

From local participatory assemblies in Curitiba to international forums in Geneva, speaking persuasively requires both logical architecture and stylistic balance. Mastering correlative conjunctions and discourse markers transforms ordinary opinions into compelling civic action. Unit outcomes · CEFR B1Understand: Analyze speeches and written civic appeals, identifying premises, supporting evidence, and conclusions.Communicate: Structure balanced arguments using Correlative Conjunctions (either... or, neither... nor, not only... but also).Produce: Deliver or write a 3-minute persuasive civic proposal for a sustainable community or workplace initiative.Interpret: Differentiate legitimate evidence-based claims from emotional oversimplifications and logical fallacies.Action: Advocate for a community resource allocation before an international panel of decision-makers. Secondary — Discourse markers of argumentation and contrast (on the one hand / on the other hand, whereas, while) and introductory focus structures (What we need is...). Optional advanced exposure — Formal logical linkers (furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, thereby) in policy documents. Previously learned structures being reinforced — Basic conjunctions and contrast linkers (and, but, because, although, despite) from A1-U08, A2-U06, and B1-U08. MERIDIAN · Volume 03 · B1 · Unit 121 MERIDIANEveryday Life · Civic Discourse & Rhetoric · Reading RoomB1 · Unit 12 Reading RoomVoices in the Agora: Curitiba's Civic Forums and the Rhetoric of SustainabilityUrban governance analysis adapted from international studies on participatory civic assemblies in Curitiba, Athens, and Geneva. In neighborhood auditoriums across Curitiba, citizen assemblies gather every month to deliberate and vote on the municipal capital budget. Originating from ancient democratic traditions like the Athenian agora, modern deliberative forums allow residents to propose urban interventions directly to public planners and civil engineers. However, having a passionate community concern is only the starting point: to secure municipal funding and win over skeptical council members, civic representatives must master the rhetoric of balanced persuasion. In these civic debates, emotional appeals alone rarely carry the day; successful speakers must present a cohesive argument supported by verifiable civic data and structured alternatives.
