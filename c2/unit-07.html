P Prof. Arthur Vance Anfitrião / Especialista
🇬🇧 British RP
Welcome, colleagues. The ethical challenge before the United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons boils down to a profound philosophical tension. If an autonomous algorithm can identify enemy armor with ninety-eight percent accuracy—far superior to a fatigued, terrified human infantryman—does a commander not have a utilitarian duty to deploy that system to minimize overall death?
D Dr. Sandra Chen Interlocutor
🌐 International English
With respect, Arthur, your premise makes a fatal category mistake by reducing human life to a target-recognition probability distribution. It is categorically incumbent upon civilized nations to recognize that the right to life is deontological, not actuarial. When a human soldier pulls a trigger, they bear moral responsibility; they can experience moral remorse; they can choose mercy. An algorithm computes loss functions; it cannot choose mercy because it has no moral consciousness.
P Prof. Arthur Vance Anfitrião / Especialista
🇬🇧 British RP
But Sandra, if human remorse comes at the price of twenty additional civilian casualties due to stray artillery fire, how can a moral theorist defend that tragic outcome?
D Dr. Sandra Chen Interlocutor
🌐 International English
Because to be killed by an algorithm is to be liquidated as a statistical nuisance. It strips the victim of their human dignity, treating them not as a person, but as an object of computational disposal. By parity of reasoning, if we ban biological weapons because their effects are uncontrollable and abhorrent to public conscience, we must ban lethal autonomous weapons. Where there is no human accountability, there can be no lawful warfare.
